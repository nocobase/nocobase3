# Interactive approval lab

## The approval center

The **Approvals** menu presents the same scenarios as business applications for the people who use them, with no lifecycle terms on screen. It has a **To-do center** and one page per business: leave, business trips, purchasing, contracts, expense claims, payments, budget authorizations, supplier onboarding, employee onboarding, product launches and notices. Its routes are under `/approval-center/`, such as `/main/approval-center/inbox`.

Each business page explains how the business works, starts a request in a dialog that shows who would handle it before it is submitted, and lists the requests waiting for the current person, the ones they started, and all of them. Opening a request, from a business page or the to-do center, shows it in a dialog over the list: its details, its approval flow — sequential steps with each person's decision and comment, or parallel tracks that open their own child request — the actions the current person may take, and its history. The identity at the top is shared by every page and kept in the browser; the chips next to it switch to the people a business involves and show how many requests wait for each of them. Only actions the lifecycle's guards allow the current person are offered, and an administrator also sees the external events the demo can simulate.

`server/approval-center/service.ts` is a read model over the lab's records: it assigns each record to its business, summarizes whom it waits for, derives each person's to-do center, and previews the route of a request from the current policies and organization. It owns no data. `GET /lifecycle-example/approval-lab/center` and `POST /lifecycle-example/approval-lab/center/preview/:key` expose it; every action goes through the same lifecycle routes as the lab.

The rest of this guide describes the lab, which shows the scenarios with their technical detail.

## The lab

The **Lifecycle Example → Approval scenarios** page runs the code in `server/approval-scenarios/` against the application's real database and jobs service. Its internal route is `/lifecycle-example/approvals`; an application mounted at `/main` opens it at `/main/lifecycle-example/approvals`.

### Start with a request

1. Sign in to the examples application. The **Example identity** selector chooses a demo persona; it does not create or sign in to another user account.
2. Choose `zhang` and click **Load sample drafts** to create one draft per scenario. This is repeatable: it does not reset existing samples. Alternatively, click **New request**, choose a scenario and edit its form. Amounts labeled cents use integer minor units.
3. Open a draft, choose **Submit** or **Start**, and execute the action. Suppliers start with a legal decision; orders wait for a simulated preparation or payment event.
4. Switch to the current approver. The todo center shows work waiting for that identity. Choose a decision and enter a reason when rejecting or returning a request.
5. Follow the stages, open parallel branches or their parent request, and inspect the final execution result. **Under the hood** contains the definition, transition log, effect runs and Mermaid diagram source. It records the identity, input, state change and version of every transition.

The todo center has **To do**, **Done by me**, **Started by me** and **Copied to me** filters. All sample records remain available in the adjacent list, including child approvals, work items, confirmations and grants. The page polls for background changes; an action also refreshes its record and the lists. Decisions submit the version the page displayed, so another window's decision causes a conflict instead of overwriting it.

### Organization, rule changes and failures

Switch to `admin` to open **Demo administration**. Manager and role overrides, inactive identities, delegations and policy versions are saved in `scenarioDemoSettings` and restored on restart. Manager and role changes affect subsequent resolution; already resolved assignees stay on their request until an explicit lifecycle action changes them. Changing `leaveVersioned` from `v1` to `v2` affects new submissions; a request in flight keeps its stored version unless an administrator explicitly migrates it.

A delegation uses `from`, `to`, ISO dates `start` and `end`, an array of request `kinds`, and `coversExisting`. For example, `{"from":"li","to":"zhao","start":"2026-01-01T00:00:00Z","end":"2099-01-01T00:00:00Z","kinds":[],"coversExisting":true}` permits `zhao` to decide for `li` within that window. Role overrides use a role name and an array of example identities. The organization list shows the current manager and roles for each active identity.

Set **Simulated outage** to `pay`, `checkCompany`, `createSupplierAccount` or `runOnboardingStep`, then execute the matching business scenario. Clear the outage before retrying. **Decline payments** makes a payment outage a business refusal instead of a transient failure. **Simulate high supplier risk** makes the registry lookup require a risk review. These controls never contact an external service. The external execution panel shows the locally stored results; payment, account, work-step and message keys prevent retries or restarts from producing duplicates.

Only `admin` may retry or cancel effect runs from **Under the hood**, and simulate the allowed system events offered in the action menu. Ordinary users still pass the lifecycle's guards. The event log includes the demo operator in its input. **Run deadlines now** checks due triggers, scheduled payments and expiring grants against real time; it does not advance the clock or bypass guards. Background jobs perform the same checks every ten seconds.

### Storage and boundaries

The migration creates business collections for leave requests, staged approval requests, coordinated requests, work items, acknowledgements, notices, orders, supplier onboardings, reimbursements, payment requests, authorization requests and budget grants. It also creates a payment reservation ledger, simulated messages and external operations, and demo settings. All lab business records use string IDs so deterministic child and grant IDs can coexist with independent UUID requests. Lifecycle history and effect runs use the existing `lifecycleExampleTransitions` and `lifecycleExampleEffectRuns` collections.

A staged approval request keeps only what is asked, where it stands and a few counters on its own row. Everything else is a row of its own, in three collections that `shared/approval-trail.ts` describes:

| Collection               | One row per              | Holds                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `scenarioApprovalStages` | stage of a round         | The plan the round was submitted under, the stage's status, when its people were chosen and the content hash it was decided on                                                     |
| `scenarioApprovalTasks`  | to-do of one person      | A decision, an opinion asked of an expert, or material asked of the applicant: who holds it and why, its status, and the answer with who gave it and on which content hash         |
| `scenarioApprovalLogs`   | step of handling a stage | What happened — a to-do assigned, decided, handed over, taken from a pool or voided, a stage activated, approved, skipped, reset or cancelled — with the transition it was part of |

A handover or reassignment closes the old to-do and opens a new one that names it, so each person's part stays on record; a return or a revision voids decisions without erasing them. Every change is written by the request's own transition, inside its transaction, so the request's version serializes concurrent decisions and a failed write leaves nothing behind. The lifecycle's transition log says how the request moved, and each approval log row names the transition it belongs to: read together, they are the request's complete timeline, which the approval center's history shows under each transition. `GET /lifecycle-example/approval-lab/approvals/:id` returns a request's stages, to-dos and log, and the to-do center finds a person's approval work by querying the to-do rows by assignee rather than reading every request.

Related-record reads and writes bind to the transition's transaction. In particular, parallel children, budget reservations and an approved grant commit with their owning transition. Notifications and external simulation results are written after commit through effects. Sample data is opt-in through the page, and is not installed as real user, department or role data.

This is a small, inspectable demonstration, not a production approval product: any signed-in user can choose its personas and inspect its records, attachment inputs hold references rather than uploading files, and the approval center still reads every request to summarize the lists. An application adapting these recipes must derive the actor from its authenticated session, authorize record reads and administrative operations, use its real organization directory and external providers, and replace demo scans with indexed queries.
